Domain 01 · Deep dive

Strategic Planning & Execution

Most districts have a strategic plan. Fewer have a mechanism that makes the plan govern anything after adoption. This domain is about the work while it is still in motion. Whether results were ultimately achieved is a separate question, handled separately.

A strategic plan is adopted with energy, printed well, and referenced constantly for about four months. Then budget season arrives, three initiatives emerge that were never in the plan, two divisions reorganize, and by spring the plan describes a district that no longer exists. Nobody decided to abandon it. It simply stopped being the thing decisions were checked against.

I. Why plans stop mattering

The failure is rarely in the plan's content. It is in what the plan was missing at adoption: the parts that turn a direction into a governing instrument.

  • Goals without owners. A goal assigned to a division is assigned to nobody. Accountability requires a name.
  • Outcomes without measures. If the evidence that would settle whether a goal was met is not defined at adoption, it will be argued about at the end, and the person with the best narrative wins.
  • Measures without milestones. An annual measure gives twelve months of ambiguity followed by a single moment of judgment. By then it is too late to intervene.
  • Milestones without a review that anyone attends. The most common failure. The plan exists, the checkpoints exist, and no standing meeting is obligated to look at them.
  • No decision rights. When a review surfaces a barrier, it is unclear who resolves it. The issue is noted, carried forward, and noted again.
The design consequence

Everything this domain does is aimed at one outcome: the plan is what decisions get checked against, in the months when nobody is watching. Not the document. The habit.

II. What a goal has to carry

Translating a priority into something that can actually govern means attaching five things to it at adoption, not later, when they become negotiable.

Anatomy of a governing goal
A priority missing any one of these will drift within a year
01
Outcome

What is actually different when this is achieved, stated in terms a person outside the district would recognize.

02
Measure

The specific evidence that settles it. Defined before work begins, including the data source and who computes it.

03
Owner

A named person, not a division. The person who answers for it in the review, whether or not they control every input.

04
Milestones

Checkpoints early enough that a problem can still be corrected inside the same year.

05 · Most often omitted
Decision point

What happens if a milestone is missed: who decides, what options exist, and by when. Defined in advance, while it is still hypothetical rather than personal.

The fifth is the one that separates a monitored plan from a governed one. Defining the response to failure while failure is still hypothetical is dramatically easier than defining it in the room where failure has just been reported.

III. Keeping work in flight visible

Milestones only matter if someone sees them slip while there is still time to act. That is a tracking discipline, not an evaluation one. The question is never how well a division performed, it is what is blocking the work right now and who can unblock it.

Three things have to stay continuously visible for that to function:

  • Status against the next milestone, stated plainly. Not activity, not effort, but whether the checkpoint will be met, and if not, by how much.
  • Dependencies between divisions. Most cross-functional failure is a dependency nobody named at the outset. One division waiting on another is invisible until it is late.
  • Decisions waiting on someone. Work stalls more often on an unmade decision than on a resource gap. Anything requiring a Superintendent or Cabinet decision should surface with options and a recommendation already prepared, not as an open question.
Where this ends and evaluation begins

This tracking is deliberately non-evaluative. Reporting that a milestone will be missed should carry no penalty. If it does, the reporting turns optimistic and the district loses its only early warning. Judging whether the work ultimately succeeded is a separate function on a separate cycle, and it belongs to the district effectiveness model. Confusing the two is how execution tracking quietly dies.

IV. Strategy without taking ownership

Cross-functional initiatives are where this function is most useful and most easily resented. The distinction has to be explicit and defended repeatedly.

This function leads
Strategy, measurement, evaluation
  • Defining what success looks like and how it will be evidenced
  • Designing the measurement approach before implementation begins
  • Running the review cadence and surfacing what it finds
  • Evaluating whether the initiative produced its intended result
  • Recommending continuation, refinement, expansion, or discontinuation
Division leaders retain
Operational ownership
  • How the work actually gets done
  • Staffing, sequencing, and day-to-day decisions
  • Their team, their budget, their professional judgment
  • The relationships with campuses and stakeholders
  • Authority to disagree with the measurement and say so

That last item matters. A measurement framework a division leader cannot contest is a framework they will quietly work around. The disagreement should happen in the design conversation, on the record, where it can improve the measure.

V. Where alignment quietly breaks

Monitoring alignment sounds abstract until you name the specific seams where plans and reality separate. These are the recurring ones.

SeamHow it driftsWhat catches it
Plan ↔ budgetA priority is adopted without funding, or funding shifts mid-year to something never in the plan.Reviewing budget amendments against plan priorities, not just against prior-year spend.
District ↔ campus plansCampus improvement plans are built on templates and timelines disconnected from district goals.Checking whether campus plans reference district priorities in a way that changed what the campus does.
Plan ↔ new initiativesInitiatives appear mid-year that are individually reasonable and collectively unplanned.A standing question at every review: which priority does this serve, and what is it displacing?
Goals ↔ assumptionsGoals rest on enrollment, staffing, or capacity assumptions that have since changed.Re-checking planning assumptions annually rather than at plan adoption only.
Plan ↔ evaluationPrograms continue by default because nobody was assigned to ask whether they worked.A scheduled evaluation calendar with named programs and dates set at adoption.

The fourth row is where this domain touches the rest of the framework most directly. Enrollment projections, capacity figures, and staffing models are planning assumptions before they are anything else. When they move, and they do, every goal resting on them needs revisiting rather than quiet obsolescence.

VI. Leading the function

This is a supervisory role, and the work described across this framework is not a solo analytical exercise. It depends on a small team doing specialized work well.

Performance Outcomes and Data

Accountability, assessment, analytics, research, and planning already sit with people who know this district's data better than any incoming executive will. The job is alignment to priorities and clearing obstacles, not redirecting expertise that already exists.

Strategic Compensation

A live system in implementation needs sustained project management, documentation discipline, and cross-functional coordination with HR, Finance, and Legal. Supporting that work is a supervision responsibility, not an oversight one.

Clear expectations, real feedback

Named outcomes, honest coaching, and addressing performance concerns directly rather than through avoidance. The same standard this framework applies to the organization applies inside the function.

Budget and compliance stewardship

Managing assigned resources against district priorities, and ensuring the function's own records, contracts, and processes meet policy and statute. Unglamorous, and the first thing noticed when it lapses.

The standard this function is held to

Everything on this page describes a discipline being asked of other divisions: named owners, defined measures, surfaced dependencies, decisions made in advance. None of it is credible unless the function asking for it operates that way internally first, visibly, and before anyone else is asked to.

A strategic plan is not a document that describes the future. It is the thing decisions get checked against in the months when nobody is watching.
