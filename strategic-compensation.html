Strategic Compensation & Measures of Excellence

The data capture year is behind us. Effectiveness Levels have been assigned and the first salary placements are live. The question is no longer whether the system works on paper, but what the first cycle revealed and whether it will hold when the protections come off.

Irving has replaced tenure-based principal pay with compensation tied to demonstrated effectiveness. The transition year is complete, ratings have been issued, and placements are in effect. That makes this the least theoretical domain in this framework and the one where attention now matters most, because the design questions are settled and the implementation questions are just beginning.

I. Where the system actually is

Three things are true at the same time right now, and they pull in different directions.

  • Principals have seen a number attached to their name. Whatever the guidebook says, the system became real the moment a rating was issued. Trust in it is being formed now, on the basis of that first experience, and first impressions of compensation systems are unusually durable.
  • Nobody has been financially harmed yet. Hold harmless means this year can only move salaries upward. The system is operating without its consequences visible, which is a genuinely useful year and also a misleading one.
  • The hardest year has not arrived. Rolling two-year averages begin next cycle, and with them the first real possibility of downward movement.
The practical read

A compensation system is not tested when it pays people more. It is tested the first time it pays someone less, and that test is one year away. Everything worth doing between now and then is preparation for it.

II. What the first cycle should tell us

The transition year existed to generate exactly this information. Whether it was examined closely is a separate question from whether it was collected, and these are the things worth knowing before the next cycle rather than after.

  • Did scores actually spread? If principals clustered into two Effectiveness Levels, the instrument is not differentiating and the compensation structure will not do what it was built to do. This is the single most important diagnostic available.
  • Where did points concentrate? If most variation traces to one or two components while the rest score uniformly, those components are effectively carrying the whole system and should be examined for whether they deserve that weight.
  • Did the accountability cap bind? A D or F rating caps a score at Progressing regardless of points earned elsewhere. How many principals it affected, and how far it moved them, determines whether it functions as a backstop or as the primary driver.
  • Do the cut points still fit? They vary by school type and are reviewed annually. The first real distribution is the first honest evidence of whether those ranges land where they were intended to.
  • What did principals conclude? Not whether they liked their rating. Whether they understood how it was produced and believed it was applied consistently. That belief is the system's actual operating capital.

III. The year the protections come off

The implementation design is deliberately conservative, and correctly so. Hold harmless in the first year, rolling two-year averages after that, and a cap of one Effectiveness Level of movement in either direction annually. Nobody experiences a sudden financial shock from a single difficult year.

But those protections do not extend indefinitely. The next cycle is when a two-year average can place a principal lower than the year before, and when the system produces its first genuine loss.

What that year needs

Every downward placement in that first cycle will be examined closely, by the principal affected and by every colleague watching. Each one needs to be traceable to documented rules, reproducible from the data, and explainable in a conversation. The work that makes that possible happens this year, not that one.

There is a second-order effect worth anticipating. The conservatism that protects principals from sharp drops also slows genuine improvement, since a principal placed low in the first cycle climbs one level at a time regardless of how quickly they improve. Whether that trade is right is a values question rather than a technical one, but it should be answered deliberately rather than discovered.

IV. The architecture, briefly

One hundred points across four components, weighted so that student outcomes and instructional leadership together carry more than appraisal alone. Component measures shift by campus type so early childhood, elementary, middle, and high school leaders are assessed on what is measurable in their setting.

Six Effectiveness Levels, four campus classifications, and cut points that vary by school type. Scoring uses three approaches deliberately: proportional conversion for T-PESS, STAAR, and the LIT rubric; standard deviation bands built from Irving's own distribution for growth measures; and target-plus-stretch for operational metrics.

The measure most worth protecting

Teacher Effectiveness is scored on the correlation between a principal's T-TESS ratings and NWEA MAP growth. It does not ask whether teachers performed well. It asks whether the principal's ratings distinguish stronger teachers from weaker ones. That matters beyond this system: a compensation model that eventually extends to teachers depends entirely on appraisal ratings meaning something, and this puts a price on rating validity before that extension happens.

V. What needs verification

Systems built by one group and implemented by another develop gaps. Not through carelessness, but through the ordinary process of people making reasonable local decisions without access to the reasoning behind the original design. They surface most cheaply in the window between a system going live and its protections expiring, which is exactly where this one sits.

These are the places worth looking first, ordered by how expensive they become if left unexamined.

Do the current business rules match the documented ones?

The first verification is the simplest and the most important: pull the calculation logic actually in use and reconcile it line by line against the guidebook. Where they differ, the question is not which is right but which was intended, and whether the change was a deliberate improvement or an undocumented workaround that has since become permanent.

Have the tiered bands been recalculated against current district data?

Standard deviation bands built from Irving's own distribution are only valid while that distribution is current. If the bands were set once and carried forward, they are measuring principals against a district that no longer exists. This is the most likely place for silent drift and one of the easiest to correct.

Cut points are reviewed annually. On what basis, and by whom?

The guidebook states cut points are evaluated annually to remain accurate and appropriately challenging. That is correct practice and also the most contestable element in the system. Without a documented methodology covering what evidence triggers a change, who decides, and how it is communicated, an annual judgment call becomes an annual argument.

Culture and climate scores participation, not experience

Five points awarded on survey completion rate. That was a deliberate first-cycle choice: establish participation before scoring results, because scoring results too early suppresses honest responses. It was always intended as a stepping stone, and the listening architecture is what completes it.

Is the audit trail sufficient to reconstruct a single principal's score?

Business rules, data sources, validation steps, calculation logic, and timelines should be documented well enough that any individual result can be rebuilt a year later by someone who was not in the room. Compensation systems are litigated in hallways long before anywhere else, and documentation is the entire defense.

VI. How it connects to everything else

Strategic Compensation is not a standalone program, and its placement in this portfolio is what makes the rest of the framework coherent.

To campus capacity. Principal tenure is a direct component of the campus capacity measure. A compensation system built to retain effective principals should show up as rising leadership stability over time. That is a testable hypothesis, and the panel data exists to test it.

To organizational health. The culture and climate component currently measures participation. The listening architecture is what would eventually let it measure experience, and the two systems are already touching through the same surveys.

To portfolio and capacity work. Reconfiguration changes campus classifications, which changes salary bands. Any consolidation decision has direct compensation consequences for the principals involved, and those need to be modeled before decisions are announced rather than discovered afterward.

To the effectiveness model. The sequencing logic here, leadership goes first and then each next layer, is exactly the principle any central office measurement should follow. Principals have already accepted an instrument that determines their pay. That is a reasonable argument that central office should accept one too.

The honest summary

The architecture is sound and the sequencing is right. What a system like this needs at this stage is not redesign but verification: confirming that what runs matches what was designed, recalculating what was always meant to be recalculated, and documenting it to the standard compensation requires. Then connecting it to the capacity, climate, and portfolio work so it stops operating as a standalone program.

A compensation system earns its authority the same way an appraisal does, by being explainable to the person it is applied to.
