Campus Capacity Measurement

A validated 0–100 measure of school organizational capacity, built entirely from data Texas districts already report. It makes visible the thing accountability ratings cannot show: whether a campus has the organizational conditions to sustain improvement. Irving campuses are already scored within the statewide panel.

A district can see how its campuses perform. It generally cannot see whether they are built to keep performing. This measure answers that second question, and it has already been run here: 37 campuses, 247 campus year observations, 2017 through 2024.

Irving sits marginally below the state mean while serving a student population roughly twenty points more economically disadvantaged than the validation sample. That context matters and should be stated whenever these figures are presented.

The finding that should drive everything else

Experience concentration is at 8.9 percent of maximum. In 206 of 247 campus-year observations (83 percent), an Irving campus scored zero points, meaning fewer than 70 percent of its teachers had five or more years of experience. Across seven years and 37 campuses, exactly one campus ever reached the top band.

Why this is the headline

Experience concentration is the strongest predictor of achievement across every specification in the statewide validation. It is the single capacity component where Irving is closest to the floor. If the district changes one thing about how it builds organizational capacity, the evidence says it should be this.

The companion indicator tells a second story. Experience distribution, whether a campus avoids concentrating inexperienced teachers relative to district average, sits at 49 percent and has been essentially flat for seven years. Roughly half of Irving campuses carry a higher share of inexperienced teachers than the district average, and the pattern has not moved. That is an internal equity question the district can answer today.

Seven years: real gains, then erosion

District mean CCI, 2018–2024
37 campuses · 247 campus-year observations

Irving gained 14.1 points of organizational capacity between 2018 and 2022, then gave back 5.4 of them. The rise was driven almost entirely by leadership stability: principal tenure moved from 23 percent of maximum in 2018 to 73 percent in 2022, the largest movement of any indicator in the panel. Teacher retention improved alongside it.

The share of campuses in the Minimal band fell from 62.9 percent to 34.3 percent over that window, then climbed back to 48.6 percent by 2024. Whatever produced the improvement was real and was not sustained.

The operational read

Capacity in Irving is volatile rather than structurally low. The district demonstrated it can move these numbers substantially, within four years, through leadership continuity. The 2023 decline suggests the gains depended on conditions that were not locked in. Understanding what changed is a first-year question worth answering precisely.

Where Irving stands on each component

Indicator performance, share of maximum · all years

Credentialing is not Irving's problem. Retention is reasonable. Leadership stability is mid-range and improving. The capacity constraint is concentrated almost entirely in one place, and it is the place the evidence says matters most.

An honest complication

Within Irving alone, the correlation between campus CCI and student achievement is approximately zero. This should be said plainly rather than omitted.

There are defensible reasons: 37 campuses is a small panel, capacity variance within a single district is compressed relative to the statewide range, and Irving's campuses are uniformly high-poverty, which removes much of the variation the statewide models exploit. The statewide relationship holds under district fixed effects, meaning it survives comparison of campuses inside the same district when pooled across more than a thousand districts. It does not follow that it will appear inside any one of them.

What that means practically

The index should be used in Irving as a measure of organizational condition, whether a campus is stable, experienced, credentialed, and led continuously, rather than as a predictor of next year's ratings. That is still the more useful thing to know when deciding where students can be absorbed, where support belongs, and whether improvement has anything to stand on.

A note on the compensation data

The panel carries Irving's Teacher Incentive Allotment status through 2023–24, which shows no district approval recorded in that window. That status may have changed since, and confirming it is an early-conversation item rather than a finding.

II. What this is not yet

Two honest qualifications, and they point in the same direction.

The panel runs through 2023 to 2024. It is built on publicly reported state data, which arrives on its own schedule, so the most recent year available is not the current one. The trend line is real and the structural pattern is stable, but this is a picture of a district as of two years ago rather than as of this morning.

Two of the six indicators are proxies. Full certification is inferred from degree status combined with compensation patterns rather than read from certification records. Turnover is inferred from year over year shifts in average teacher experience rather than observed from actual departures. Both are defensible methodological choices when public data is all you have. Neither is what you would use if you had the real thing.

Why that argues for the measure rather than against it

The starkest finding on this page, experience concentration at 8.9 percent of maximum, was produced by the blurry version. A proxy that imprecise still surfaced a pattern that clear. Sharpening the inputs does not soften that finding. It makes it more precise, more current, and considerably harder to argue with.

What district access changes

  • Currency. Internal personnel and staffing data exists in real time rather than on a public release cycle. Capacity becomes a current measure updated annually rather than a retrospective one.
  • Precision. Actual certification records replace the compensation proxy. Actual personnel transactions replace inferred turnover, which also distinguishes a retirement from a resignation from a transfer, three very different signals currently collapsed into one.
  • Resolution. Distribution indicators computed against real campus assignments rather than reported averages, which is what makes the internal equity question answerable at the level it actually operates.
  • Additional signal. Vacancy duration, time to fill, internal transfer patterns, and mid year departures are all capacity indicators that simply do not exist in public data and could be tested for whether they improve the measure.
  • Validation against judgment. The most useful test of any index is whether it agrees with what experienced people already believe about their campuses. That conversation is only possible from inside.

The instrument was designed to work from data every Texas district already reports, which is what makes it usable anywhere. That was a deliberate constraint, not a limitation of the underlying method. Applied to a single district with full access to its own records, the same approach produces something meaningfully better than what is shown here.

III. Why this is the right thing to measure

Accountability ratings report outcomes. They say nothing about whether a campus has the organizational memory, professional standardization, and leadership continuity that make those outcomes reproducible, or whether a rating was carried by a single strong cohort and is about to fall.

Two campuses can hold the same rating for entirely different reasons. One has a stable principal, a majority of experienced teachers, and low turnover. The other has a first-year principal, a teaching staff that turns over annually, and results that will not survive the next departure. Outcome data treats them identically. Every practical decision a district makes, from where to send students during consolidation to where to direct support to which campuses can absorb change, treats them very differently, or should.

The instrument

The Campus Capacity Index is a formative composite scored 0–100 from six indicators across three theoretical dimensions. It was developed and validated against 48,620 Texas campus-year observations spanning 8,605 campuses and seven academic years, using only publicly reported administrative data.

Because it is formative, the six indicators combine to produce capacity rather than reflecting one underlying trait. A campus with experienced teachers but constant principal turnover genuinely has different capacity than one with the reverse. The indicators are contributors, not substitutes, which is why the measure is judged on whether it predicts what schools care about, not on whether its items correlate with one another.

IV. How the score is built

Validity rests on criterion performance and on whether organizational profiles occurring naturally in the data align with the index's categories. Latent class analysis agrees with CCI readiness levels on 58 to 64 percent of campuses. Associations with achievement hold under district fixed effects, meaning capacity differences matter even among campuses operating under the same district policies and resource constraints, which is precisely the comparison a district cares about.

The finding that matters most operationally

Teacher experience concentration is the strongest predictor across every specification tested. Strategic concentration of experienced teachers outperforms spreading them thin. That is a direct, testable claim about how a district should staff, and it runs against the instinct to distribute strong teachers evenly across every campus.

V. What it changes for Irving

The findings above are only useful if they change a decision. These are the decisions they touch, all of which this district is going to make regardless.

A defensible receiving-campus criterion

When students move between campuses, the receiving campus should have the organizational conditions to absorb them. Physical space is the easy test. Capacity is the real one, and a published index gives the district a criterion it can defend publicly rather than an assertion it has to argue.

Equity that is measured, not asserted

Two of the six indicators measure whether the district concentrates inexperienced or uncredentialed teachers at particular campuses. That is an internal equity question most districts never quantify, and it is answerable today from data already reported.

Early warning ahead of accountability

Capacity predicts achievement. A campus whose capacity is eroding through leadership turnover or experience loss is signaling a rating problem well before the rating arrives. That is a support-targeting window most districts miss entirely.

A capacity lens on Strategic Compensation

Performance-based compensation appears to raise achievement substantially through organizational capacity development rather than through incentive effects alone. If that mechanism holds, capacity is the intermediate outcome a district should be tracking to know whether its compensation program is working.

Staffing structure, grounded

The experience concentration finding gives a tiered career structure an empirical foundation. Placement decisions during any reconfiguration can be governed by what demonstrably builds capacity rather than by seniority alone.

Trend without new burden

Built from data the district already reports, and already computed. A seven-year capacity trend line exists for every Irving campus right now, with no new survey, form, or data request required to begin.

VI. Limits and honest caveats

  • The associations are conditional, not causal. The index predicts achievement; it does not establish that raising a score causes achievement to rise.
  • It measures organizational conditions, not instruction. A high-capacity campus is better positioned to sustain good teaching. It is not evidence that good teaching is occurring.
  • It should inform decisions, not make them. Any index used mechanically will eventually produce a defensible number and an indefensible decision. This one belongs alongside judgment, not in place of it.
Why it belongs in this seat

Capacity measurement connects directly to portfolio decisions, staffing structure, compensation design, and campus support targeting. Those threads sit in four different departments today. The value of measuring capacity is realized only where someone is accountable for all four at once.

A district can see how its campuses perform. Knowing whether they are built to keep performing is a different question.
